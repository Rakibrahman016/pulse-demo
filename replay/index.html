How the mask finds an apnea without an airflow sensor

Breathing stops or gets shallow

The sleep lab sees this with a sensor under the nose. The mask has nothing under the nose, so it watches what the pause causes.

Oxygen falls

Some seconds later the blood oxygen (SpO2) dips. This is the strongest single clue: in our tests SpO2 carries most of the information.

The body reacts

Breathing restarts with a brief arousal: the pulse speeds up, brain waves change, and there is often a snort or gasp. The model reads all of these every 30 seconds.

What is real on this page

Every trace is a real overnight recording from the UCD Sleep Apnea Database (PhysioNet, St Vincent's University Hospital, Dublin). The orange events were scored by sleep technologists from the full lab recording, including airflow. The mask model never sees airflow: it uses only the channels the mask will have (oxygen, pulse, EEG/EOG, head position, sound).

Each night is scored by a model trained on other patients only (5-fold cross-validation split by patient), so the estimate is what the mask would say about a new person. The night order is the database order, not a selection of good examples.

These are sleep-lab sensors standing in for the mask: a finger oximeter instead of the forehead sensor, central EEG instead of a forehead electrode. This is a research prototype, not a medical device, and 25 patients is a small sample.
